Back in Baltimore

By the early 1960s, Eli “Ted” Hanover was moving from maritime and union work into a new chapter of Baltimore life. His former experience in the ring became the foundation for training, managing, and eventually promoting fighters. The transition was gradual rather than a single documented turning point. Exactly how he acquired the Jewel Box—and how his union colleagues, relatives, and boxing associates contributed—remains an important research question.

The Jewel Box and the gym upstairs

On East Baltimore Street’s entertainment district, known as “The Block,” Eli operated the Jewel Box nightclub. Above the club was a boxing gym. The contrast became central to recollections of him: nightlife below and the disciplined work of young boxers above. The existence and location of this gym are reported in the biographical research; its daily routines, the fighters who trained there, and the people who kept it running deserve testimony from firsthand witnesses.

From training fighters to promoting fights

The project biography reports Eli’s first promoted show in 1965 and describes his work as a licensed promoter by the middle of the decade. He staged local fight cards and worked to draw audiences to Baltimore boxing. We should establish exact dates, licensed partners, opponents, venues, and results from programs, press notices, commission files, and ticket stubs rather than treating the reported 1965 starting date as a fully verified milestone.

A city caught between two venues

The research describes the practical problem facing Eli: Steelworkers Hall was intimate but financially limited, while the Baltimore Civic Center offered far more seats than a local fight card could reliably fill. He reportedly considered developing a roughly 3,000-seat venue to bridge that gap. The idea speaks to the economic realities of promoting: rent, guarantees, purses, advertising, commissions, and ticket sales mattered as much as the fights themselves. We have not yet obtained financial records or a formal proposal for the arena.

Fighters, matchmakers, and the people behind the card

Preserved promotional posters identify Eli Hanover as promoter and Eddie Hrica as matchmaker. Other archival materials connect this period to local fighters and participants including Leo “Kid” Saenz, although each person’s precise relationship to a particular event should be checked against contemporary records. A bout was never made by the promoter alone: trainers, matchmakers, officials, ticket sellers, seconds, families, and fans all took part in the local boxing community.

The cost of an empty arena

The research recounts a disappointing Civic Center promotion followed by a break from staging fights lasting approximately eighteen months. That interval and the underlying event date require confirmation, and we should not assume the setback was the only reason for his pause. Relatives have also raised questions about Eli’s family responsibilities and later health. A complete account should give room to both the public business circumstances and the experiences of those close to him.

His return to promoting

According to the biographical account, Eli returned to putting on fights in the early 1970s. His ambitions were considerable, but the record suggests financial success was difficult. What brought him back—attachment to the sport, support for specific fighters, a desire to provide a stage for Baltimore talent, or something more personal—is best answered by people who worked alongside him.

More than the next fight

The promoter’s story also belongs to the Hanover family. What did Frances and the children see of this public life? Were there nights when the whole family attended a show, or stretches when the costs of promotion affected home life? As we investigate the public record, we should also preserve the domestic story without inventing private conversations or motivations. Chapter 5 will follow Eli’s later years and the recognition he received after his death.
